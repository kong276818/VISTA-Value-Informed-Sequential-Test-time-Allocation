\begin{abstract}
Reasoning language models can spend widely different amounts of test-time
compute before producing an answer, yet deployment systems still require a rule
for deciding how much reasoning is worth performing. A fixed global budget
ignores query-level heterogeneity, whereas many adaptive stopping methods rely
on confidence, entropy, answer stability, or other signals observed along the
current trajectory. Such signals may indicate that reasoning has stabilised,
but they do not by themselves establish whether the current answer is correct
or whether additional reasoning would improve it.

We study test-time compute selection in a deployment regime with no separate
held-out calibration set but with outcomes that become available after a delay.
The setting involves three interlocking obstacles: (i)~\emph{correctness
non-identifiability}---without structural assumptions, current correctness is
not identified by the observable reasoning history alone, as two
data-generating processes can agree on every observable quantity while
assigning opposite correctness; (ii)~the \emph{missing continuation
counterfactual}---when a query is stopped at an intermediate checkpoint, the
prediction a longer trajectory would have produced is unobserved; and
(iii)~the absence of a held-out calibration split, which rules out standard
offline stopping-rule fitting. We address the first obstacle by replacing
current correctness with the \emph{marginal value of thinking}, defined as the
expected improvement in a proper scoring loss from an additional reasoning
block, net of its compute cost.

Building on this formulation, we introduce \textbf{VISTA}, a held-out-free
prequential framework for reasoning-budget selection. VISTA randomly audits a
subset of deployment queries to reveal longer-budget counterfactuals, uses
delayed outcomes to evaluate candidate stopping policies, and maintains
anytime-valid confidence sequences while the deployment stream evolves.
The method reserves no separate calibration split, performs no language-model
parameter update, and explicitly charges the compute consumed by exploration.

Before evaluating the adaptive procedure itself, we test the more fundamental
premise on which it depends: whether the value of additional reasoning varies
meaningfully across queries and budgets. We therefore audit accuracy, Brier
score, negative log-likelihood, confidence, and answer transitions across a
fixed reasoning-budget grid, quantify the gap between the best fixed budget and
an item-adaptive oracle, and examine whether observable prefix signals predict
future continuation value.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Reasoning language models have turned inference-time computation into a
decision variable. For the same query, a model may be allowed to reason for a
few hundred tokens or for several thousand before committing to an answer.
Increasing this budget provides additional opportunities to reconsider an
intermediate conclusion, recover from an error, or solve a difficult problem.
It can also consume computation without changing the answer, or even replace a
previously correct answer with an incorrect one. The central deployment
question is therefore not simply whether more reasoning improves average
performance, but \emph{when an additional block of reasoning is worth its cost
for the current query}.

The simplest solution is to choose one global reasoning budget and apply it to
every query. Such a policy is efficient only when queries benefit from
additional reasoning in approximately the same way. If some problems are
resolved reliably with short trajectories while others benefit from
substantially more computation, a fixed budget must either waste compute on
the former or under-allocate it to the latter. The potential value of adaptive
test-time computation therefore rests on a basic empirical premise:
\emph{the value of additional reasoning must vary meaningfully across queries
and across stages of a trajectory}. Whether this opportunity exists should be
measured rather than assumed.

A natural alternative is to inspect the current trajectory and stop when the
model appears sufficiently certain. Existing approaches use quantities such as
predictive confidence, entropy, self-consistency, answer convergence, or the
stability of intermediate predictions. These quantities can provide useful
information about the trajectory, but they do not directly answer the compute
allocation question. A high-confidence answer can still be wrong and may be
corrected by further reasoning. Conversely, even a currently correct answer
can be altered by continuation. The decision to continue therefore depends not
only on the state of the current prediction, but on the \emph{counterfactual
value of additional computation}.

This distinction is particularly important when a labeled calibration set is
not available at deployment time. Let $\Hist_{\budget}$ denote everything
observable from a reasoning trajectory up to budget $\budget$, including the
query, generated reasoning, logits, current answer, and predictive
distribution. Without an additional assumption linking these observables to
the unknown ground-truth label, correctness is not identified by
$\Hist_{\budget}$. In Section~\ref{sec:impossibility}, we formalize this point
with a two-world construction: the same distribution of observable histories
can coexist with opposite correctness assignments. Consequently, no function
of an unlabeled single trajectory can generally provide non-trivial
distribution-free control of current correctness. Confidence, stability, and
self-consistency may characterize properties of the model trajectory, but they
cannot by themselves certify that the trajectory is correct.

Rather than constructing a more elaborate proxy for current correctness, we
change the target of the decision. Consider a globally fixed grid of reasoning
budgets
\begin{equation}
  \budget_1 < \budget_2 < \cdots < \budget_K .
\end{equation}
For item $i$, let $\Brier_{i,j}$ denote a proper scoring loss at checkpoint
$\budget_j$, and let $\cost_{i,j}$ denote its realised compute. We define the
marginal value of continuing from $\budget_j$ to $\budget_{j+1}$ as
\begin{equation}
  \margval_j(\lambda)
  =
  \Exp\!\left[\Brier_{i,j}-\Brier_{i,j+1}\right]
  -
  \lambda\,\Exp\!\left[\cost_{i,j+1}-\cost_{i,j}\right],
  \label{eq:intro-marginal-value}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ is a user-specified exchange rate between predictive
quality and compute. The resulting decision is not
\emph{``Is the current answer correct?''}, but
\emph{``Is another block of reasoning worth its cost?''}

This reformulation is useful because continuation value can be learned from
outcomes that arrive after the original deployment decision has already been
made. Many reasoning workloads naturally provide such delayed but verifiable
feedback. Mathematical answers can eventually be checked, programs can be
executed against tests, formal proofs can be verified, and multiple-choice
decisions can later receive labels. These settings make it possible to update
a reasoning-budget policy from the deployment stream itself, without reserving
a separate labeled subset purely for calibration. We refer to this setting as
\emph{held-out-free prequential compute selection}.

The remaining difficulty is counterfactual. When a policy stops an item at
budget $\budget_j$, the prediction that would have been obtained at
$\budget_{j+1}$ is normally unobserved. We address this using randomized
trajectory audits: a controlled subset of arriving queries is continued to a
longer or maximum budget, revealing the sequence of checkpoint predictions
that candidate stopping policies would have encountered. Once the delayed label
arrives, these audited trajectories can be used to evaluate the predictive
loss and compute consumption of multiple policies on the same item.

We introduce \textbf{VISTA}---\emph{Value-Informed Sequential Test-time
Allocation}---for this purpose. VISTA combines randomized
continuation, importance-weighted evaluation, and simultaneous anytime-valid
confidence sequences to compare a pre-specified family of reasoning-budget
policies using deployment feedback. The procedure does not reserve a held-out
calibration split and does not update model parameters or train an auxiliary
predictor. Importantly, the cost of randomized exploration is included in the
total compute accounting; a stopping procedure that saves compute only by
excluding the cost of learning its policy would provide a misleading deployment
comparison.

The proposed framework is useful only if adaptive compute allocation has
something to exploit. We therefore place the empirical audit before the final
evaluation of VISTA. First, we measure how reasoning budget changes accuracy,
Brier score, negative log-likelihood, predictive confidence, and answer
transitions such as correct-to-wrong and wrong-to-correct flips. Second, we
compare the best global fixed budget with an item-adaptive oracle to quantify
the maximum compute-selection opportunity in each model--dataset setting.
Third, we test whether information observable at an intermediate checkpoint is
predictive of the gain from an additional reasoning block.

In summary, the framework addresses three named obstacles that compound in the
target regime: \emph{correctness non-identifiability}
(Section~\ref{sec:impossibility}), the \emph{missing continuation
counterfactual} (addressed by randomized full-trajectory auditing), and the
\emph{absence of a held-out calibration split} (addressed by prequential
updating with delayed feedback).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Non-identifiability of label-free correctness.}
We formalize why current correctness cannot, without additional structural
assumptions, be identified from an unlabeled single reasoning trajectory,
motivating the shift from correctness to continuation value as the stopping
target.

\item \textbf{Marginal-value formulation of test-time reasoning.}
We formulate each additional reasoning block as a discrete continuation
decision whose value combines improvement in a proper prediction loss with its
realised compute cost, admitting a reliability--resolution decomposition.

\item \textbf{Held-out-free prequential budget selection.}
We introduce VISTA, combining randomized trajectory audits, delayed
deployment feedback, importance-weighted policy evaluation, and anytime-valid
confidence sequences without reserving a separate calibration split.

\item \textbf{Empirical audit and component analysis.}
We evaluate how reasoning budget affects predictive quality and answer
dynamics across datasets, quantify item-level hindsight headroom relative to
a fixed-budget baseline, test whether observable prefix signals predict future
continuation value, and characterise the behaviour of individual VISTA
components via CPU replay experiments on stored checkpoint tables.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Marginal returns of test-time compute.}
The observation that additional reasoning can exhibit diminishing or negative
returns motivates adaptive allocation of inference-time computation. Prior work
has primarily characterised such behaviour retrospectively on labelled
benchmarks or designed heuristics for early termination. We treat the value of
continuation as a deployment-time quantity that must be estimated and updated
online.

\paragraph{Reasoning and calibration.}
Increasing the reasoning budget can change not only top-1 correctness but also
the predictive distribution. Rather than assuming that longer reasoning
universally improves or degrades calibration, we treat the relationship between
reasoning budget and proper scoring loss as an empirical question measured
across budgets, models, and tasks.

\paragraph{Training-free early exit.}
Several approaches terminate reasoning without additional model training using
trial answers, entropy, confidence trajectories, answer convergence, or
stability of intermediate predictions. Such signals may indicate trajectory
stabilisation, but without additional assumptions they do not resolve the
non-identifiability established in
Proposition~\ref{prop:impossibility}.

\paragraph{Statistical guarantees for stopping.}
Distribution-free risk control, calibration procedures, and sequential testing
provide principled mechanisms for selecting stopping rules, but typically rely
on labelled calibration data sampled before deployment. We study a regime where
no calibration split is reserved and labels arrive sequentially.

\paragraph{Anytime-valid inference.}
Confidence sequences and e-processes provide guarantees that remain valid under
continuous monitoring and optional stopping, making them natural for
deployment-time budget selection where evidence accumulates and policy updates
can occur at any time.

\section{Preliminaries and an Impossibility Result}
\label{sec:impossibility}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Fix a query $x$ with ground-truth answer $y^{\star}$. A reasoning model
produces a trajectory $r_{1:\Bmax}$, and at each checkpoint $\budget$ we may
force termination and read off a candidate answer $\hat{y}_{\budget}$ together
with a predictive distribution $\hat{p}_{\budget} \in \Delta^{C-1}$. Write the
observable history as
\begin{equation}
  \Hist_{\budget} =
  \bigl(x,\;r_{1:\budget},\;\mathrm{logits}_{1:\budget},\;
  \hat{y}_{\budget},\;\hat{p}_{\budget}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the unobserved correctness indicator as
$Y_{\budget} = \Ind\{\hat{y}_{\budget} = y^{\star}\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Non-identifiability of label-free correctness]
\label{prop:impossibility}
Let $f$ be any measurable estimator
$\hat{a}_{\budget} = f(\Hist_{\budget})$.
There exist two joint distributions $\Prob_{0},\Prob_{1}$ over
$(\Hist_{\budget},Y_{\budget})$ with identical marginals on $\Hist_{\budget}$
such that $Y_{\budget}=0$ a.s.\ under $\Prob_{0}$ and $Y_{\budget}=1$
a.s.\ under $\Prob_{1}$. Consequently, for every $\Hist_{\budget}$,
\begin{equation}
  \max\Bigl\{|\hat{a}_{\budget}-0|,\;|\hat{a}_{\budget}-1|\Bigr\}
  \geq \tfrac{1}{2},
\end{equation}
and no non-trivial distribution-free finite-sample lower bound on
$\Prob(Y_{\budget}=1\mid\Hist_{\budget})$ exists.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Construct $\Prob_{1}$ from $\Prob_{0}$ by relabelling $y^{\star}$ to agree
with $\hat{y}_{\budget}$ on every realisation. Since $y^{\star}$ enters
$\Hist_{\budget}$ nowhere, the marginal law of $\Hist_{\budget}$ is unchanged,
so any estimator or stopping rule measurable with respect to $\Hist_{\budget}$
behaves identically under both distributions. The bound follows pointwise.

\end{proof}

The obstruction is informational: collecting additional unlabelled trajectories
cannot remove it without assumptions connecting observable model behaviour to
ground-truth correctness. Self-consistency, answer stability, and related
trajectory-based quantities are descriptive signals but do not constitute
correctness guarantees.

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:impossibility} does not preclude label-free detection of
\emph{trajectory stabilisation} such as answer convergence, entropy plateaus,
or representational saturation. It also does not apply to trained probes that
condition on labelled supervision, or to procedures that use a separate labeled
calibration set---both of which supply additional information beyond the raw
trajectory. The obstruction is specific to estimators that are simultaneously
(a)~distribution-free, (b)~label-free at deployment time, and (c)~restricted to
a single unlabeled trajectory. Supplying any one of labelled examples, a
distributional assumption, or repeated trajectories from the same query opens
avenues not covered by this result.
\end{remark}

\section{Continuation Value and Budget Selection}
\label{sec:formulation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Loss}

Let $\budget_1 < \cdots < \budget_K = \Bmax$ be a globally fixed budget grid.
For item $i$ at checkpoint $j$ we use the \emph{summed} multiclass Brier score
\begin{equation}
  \Brier_{i,j}
  = \bigl\lVert\hat{p}_{i,j} - e_{y_i}\bigr\rVert_2^2
  = \sum_{c=1}^{C}\bigl(\hat{p}_{i,j,c} - \mathbf{1}_{c=y_i}\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
where the sum over classes is not divided by $C$.  This is the standard
proper-scoring form; dividing by $C$ would yield a differently scaled variant
with different decomposition constants.
and define the deployment loss
\begin{equation}
  \lossfn^{(\lambda)}_{i,j} = \Brier_{i,j} + \lambda\,\cost_{i,j},
  \qquad \lambda\geq 0,
\end{equation}
where $\cost_{i,j}$ is realised compute and $\lambda$ is a user-specified
exchange rate between predictive quality and compute, reported over a range
of values rather than at a single operating point.

\subsection{Marginal value and its decomposition}

\begin{definition}[Marginal value of a reasoning block]
The marginal value of continuing from checkpoint $j$ to $j+1$ is
\begin{equation}
  \margval_j(\lambda)
  =
  \Exp\!\left[\Brier_{i,j}-\Brier_{i,j+1}\right]
  -\lambda\,\Exp\!\left[\cost_{i,j+1}-\cost_{i,j}\right].
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Applying the reliability--resolution--uncertainty decomposition
$\Exp[\Brier_j] = \mathrm{REL}_j - \mathrm{RES}_j + \mathrm{UNC}$
and noting that UNC cancels across budgets yields
\begin{equation}
  \margval_j(\lambda)
  =
  \underbrace{\bigl(\mathrm{RES}_{j+1}-\mathrm{RES}_j\bigr)}_{\text{change in resolution}}
  -
  \underbrace{\bigl(\mathrm{REL}_{j+1}-\mathrm{REL}_j\bigr)}_{\text{change in reliability error}}
  -\lambda\,\Delta\cost_j.
  \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:decomposition} separates two ways an additional reasoning
block can change probabilistic quality. Whether further reasoning tends to
improve accuracy while degrading calibration, improve both, or degrade both is
an empirical question that may vary across models, datasets, and budget
intervals. We treat the sign and magnitude of each component as a measurable
outcome rather than a structural claim.

\section{VISTA}
\label{sec:method}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Scope}

\begin{assumption}[Delayed verifiable feedback]
\label{asm:feedback}
For each item $i$, the outcome $y_i$ becomes available after a finite delay
through exact-answer matching, program execution, formal verification, or
post-hoc adjudication.
\end{assumption}

Tasks with open-ended generation and no eventual feedback lie outside this
setting.

\subsection{Randomized full-trajectory audits}

To observe counterfactual predictions at larger budgets, we randomly select
items for full-trajectory auditing:
\begin{equation}
  A_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_i).
\end{equation}
Audited items ($A_i=1$) are run through $\Bmax$ and all checkpoint predictions
retained. For policy $\pi$ with stopping checkpoint $\tau_\pi(\Hist_i)$,
define the realised loss
\begin{equation}
  \ell_i(\pi) = \Brier_{i,\tau_\pi} + \lambda\,\cost_{i,\tau_\pi}.
\end{equation}
The importance-weighted comparison against a reference policy
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is
\begin{equation}
  X_i(\pi)
  = \frac{A_i}{\rho_i}
    \bigl[\ell_i(\pi) - \ell_i(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\bigr],
  \label{eq:policy-ipw}
\end{equation}
with $\Exp[X_i(\pi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}] = R(\pi)-R(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$
where $R(\pi)=\Exp[\ell_i(\pi)]$. The audit probability $\rho_i$ may depend
on past observations, provided it is set before the current outcome is
revealed. All audit computation counts toward reported total compute.

\subsection{Anytime-valid policy evaluation}

We maintain simultaneous confidence sequences for the excess risk of every
policy relative to the reference. For finite family $\Pi$, the guarantee is
\begin{equation}
  \Prob\!\Bigl(
    \forall n,\;\forall\pi\in\Pi:\;
    R(\pi)-R(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
    \in[\underline{\Delta}_{\pi,n},\,\overline{\Delta}_{\pi,n}]
  \Bigr) \geq 1-\alpha,
\end{equation}
constructed via bounded empirical-Bernstein sequences or betting-based methods
with simultaneous coverage across all policies.

\subsection{Policy family}

Candidate policies include fixed-budget rules, predictive-confidence
thresholds, entropy thresholds, answer-stability rules, top-1/top-2 margin
thresholds, and confidence-slope thresholds. A generic threshold policy is
\begin{equation}
  \tau_\theta(\Hist_i) = \min\{j : s_{i,j}\leq\theta_j\},
  \qquad \theta\in\Theta,\quad|\Theta|<\infty.
\end{equation}
Every audited trajectory permits reconstruction of the stopping checkpoint and
realised loss for every member of this finite family.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{VISTA: prequential reasoning-budget selection}
\label{alg:mvtcs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require budget grid $\{\budget_j\}_{j=1}^{K}$, policy family $\Pi$,
         exchange rate $\lambda$, confidence level $\alpha$,
         audit schedule $\{\rho_i\}$
\State Initialise confidence sequences for all $\pi\in\Pi$
\For{each arriving item $i$}
  \State Select the currently preferred active policy $\pi_i$
  \State Execute reasoning process according to $\pi_i$
  \State Draw $A_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_i)$ before observing outcome
  \If{$A_i=1$}
    \State Continue item to $\Bmax$; retain all checkpoint predictions
  \EndIf
  \State Deploy the answer produced by $\pi_i$
  \State \textbf{When $y_i$ arrives:}
  \If{$A_i=1$}
    \State Reconstruct $\ell_i(\pi)$ for all $\pi\in\Pi$ from checkpoint predictions
    \State Form importance-weighted risk differences via Eq.~\eqref{eq:policy-ipw}
    \State Update confidence sequences for all $\pi\in\Pi$
    \State Identify currently preferred policy $\pi_{i+1}$ from updated sequences
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{What is and is not learned}

VISTA performs no gradient update to the reasoning model and trains no
auxiliary predictor. It does learn from the deployment stream: policy-risk
estimates, confidence sequences, and the active candidate set are
data-dependent. We therefore call the procedure \emph{held-out-free} rather
than \emph{training-free}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Models and infrastructure}

The primary model is \textbf{Qwen3-8B} (\texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-8B}), an
8-billion-parameter model with a native chain-of-thought thinking mode.
All inference uses bfloat16 weights and bfloat16 key--value caches
(\texttt{kv\_cache\_dtype=auto}); fp8\_e5m2 KV quantisation is excluded
after a diagnostic run found top-1 answer-token changes on 4/50 prompts
(8\%) on the same GPU, a corruption level that would mask sub-0.5\,pp Brier
differences.

Experiments run on a single workstation with one NVIDIA RTX~4090 (sm\_89,
Ada) and two RTX~3090 GPUs (sm\_86, Ampere; 24\,GB each, no NVLink).
Models at the 8B scale fit on a single 24\,GB card in BF16, so inference
uses independent single-GPU processes with no tensor parallelism; three
such processes can run simultaneously to maximise throughput.

Before pooling probability measurements across devices, logit reproducibility
was verified diagnostically. The two RTX~3090 cards produce byte-identical
outputs (max\,$|\Delta\text{logprob}|=0.065$\,at $b=8192$). RTX~4090 and
RTX~3090 show non-negligible differences (max\,$|\Delta\text{logprob}|=0.374$
on BF16), consistent with architecture-level kernel differences between
sm\_89 and sm\_86. Accordingly, each (model~$\times$~dataset) cell is
run on a single GPU architecture and probability measurements are
never pooled across architectures.

\subsection{Datasets}

The evaluation uses four multiple-choice benchmarks: MMLU-Pro ($n=1000$,
up to 10 options), ARC-Challenge ($n=1000$, 4 options), MedMCQA ($n=500$,
4 options), and MedQA-USMLE ($n=500$, 4 options). All provide exact labels
and a fixed option set, which is required for computing Brier score and NLL
from forced answer-cue logits.

\subsection{Budget grid, forcing protocol, and metric extraction}

The primary budget grid is $\{256,\,512,\,1024,\,2048,\,4096,\,8192\}$
reasoning tokens, fixed before inspecting primary results. We record the
natural termination point of each trajectory and distinguish natural stopping
from forced checkpoint evaluation; forced termination is treated as an explicit
intervention rather than implicitly equated to an independently generated
short-budget trajectory.

At each forced checkpoint a standardised answer-cue string
(\texttt{\textbackslash n</think>\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe answer
is **}) is appended, and the model's next-token log-probabilities over bare
option tokens (A--J, token IDs 32--41) are extracted. The predictive
distribution $\hat{p}_{i,j}$ is formed by selecting those positions and
applying softmax. In the diagnostic sample, the target option token ranks
first with probability exceeding 0.984, confirming stable elicitation.
Brier score and NLL are computed post-hoc from these stored logit vectors;
no re-inference is performed after the initial generation pass.

\subsection{Gate~0: opportunity}

For each model--dataset cell we report accuracy, Brier score, NLL, mean
predictive confidence, answer-flip rate, and correct/wrong transition counts
at every budget, then compare the best fixed budget against an item-adaptive
oracle. The primary criterion is whether the oracle reduces compute by at
least $10\%$ relative to the best fixed budget under matched predictive
quality.

\subsection{Gate~1: predictability of continuation value}

For each edge $j\!\rightarrow\!j{+}1$, define item-level gain
$G_{i,j} = \Brier_{i,j}-\Brier_{i,j+1}$.
We evaluate observable signals (confidence, entropy, top-1/top-2 margin,
answer stability, confidence slope) via
$\mathrm{AUC}(s_{i,j},\Ind\{G_{i,j}>0\})$
and Spearman $\rho$ between $s_{i,j}$ and $G_{i,j}$.
The pre-specified threshold is $\mathrm{AUC}\geq0.60$ or
$|\rho_{\mathrm{Spearman}}|\geq0.30$ on a held-out setting; failure terminates
the instance-adaptive branch.

\subsection{Baselines and metrics}

We compare fixed-budget policies, training-free stopping signals, calibration-
based procedures with a separate labeled split, and item-adaptive oracles
(opportunity measurement only). Primary metrics are multiclass Brier score,
NLL, accuracy, total reasoning tokens, wall-clock latency, total compute
including audit overhead, and token savings at matched accuracy or Brier score.

\subsection{Ablation design}
\label{sec:ablation-design}

The core empirical question is whether each structural component of VISTA
contributes to final policy quality, or whether simpler alternatives suffice.
All ablations are replay experiments on the stored checkpoint tables; no
additional GPU inference is required (except the forced-termination protocol,
which requires independent fixed-budget trajectories and is treated as a
measurement-validity diagnostic).

\paragraph{Essential ablations.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} lists the five essential ablated variants.

\emph{(A1) Audit weighting correction.}
``Naive'' policy risk is estimated directly from observed trajectories without
correcting for the randomized audit probability $\rho$: each trajectory
is counted at face value regardless of how likely it was to be observed.
``IPW'' policy risk divides each audited observation by $\rho$, restoring
the target full-population estimand.
Ignoring the observation probability attenuates the estimated risk difference
approximately in proportion to the audit rate; inverse-probability weighting
recovers the intended estimand.
\TODO{A stronger counterfactual-missingness test comparing IPW against a
hold-out of \emph{naturally stopped} items (not uniformly audited ones)
is pending.}

\emph{(A2) Anytime-valid confidence sequences.}
``VISTA w/o CS'' replaces confidence sequences with greedy policy selection
based on the running empirical mean, accepting the first policy whose estimated
risk advantage exceeds a fixed threshold without a coverage guarantee.
The expected consequence is inflated false switching under repeated
monitoring: because the empirical mean is tested repeatedly as the stream
evolves, the effective type-I error exceeds the nominal level.
We evaluate by replaying the deployment stream over multiple random
permutations and reporting the fraction of replays in which each method makes
an incorrect policy decision.

\emph{(A3) Held-out-free prequential update.}
``Fixed calibration'' treats the first $N_\text{cal}$ arriving items as a
labeled calibration set, fits a static stopping policy on them, and freezes
it for the remainder of the stream.
Under stationarity this is a competitive baseline.
Under distribution shift it degrades as the calibration set becomes
unrepresentative.
We evaluate on both stationary streams and controlled shift conditions; the
shift experiments appear in Section~\ref{sec:shift}.

\emph{(A4) Brier objective vs.\ accuracy-only.}
``Accuracy-only'' replaces the summed multiclass Brier in the deployment loss
$\lossfn^{(\lambda)}$ with a 0--1 loss $\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}\neq y]$.
Different predictive objectives may prefer different reasoning budgets,
making objective choice a first-order decision for any budget-selection policy.
We compare the budget at which each objective (Brier, accuracy, NLL) is
minimised and report confidently-wrong rates
($\max_k \hat{p}_k \geq \tau$, $\tau=0.90$, on incorrect predictions)
alongside standard accuracy and Brier.

\emph{(A5) Instance-adaptive vs.\ global policy.}
``Global VISTA'' updates a single per-deployment-stream preferred budget
without using per-item prefix signals.
The comparison to full VISTA isolates the incremental value of item-level
stopping over dataset-level budget adaptation.
If global adaptation captures most of the oracle gap, the emphasis of the
paper should shift from instance-adaptive stopping to prequential global
compute selection.

\paragraph{Additional ablations.}
\emph{(A6) Prefix-signal ablation.}
Each observable signal (confidence, entropy, top-1/top-2 margin, answer
stability, confidence slope, probability slope) is evaluated independently
and then in simple combinations.
The AUC and Spearman $\rho$ reported in Gate~1
(Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) serve as the primary diagnostic; policy results
under each single signal are reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-signals}.

\emph{(A7) Audit probability $\rho$ sensitivity.}
We evaluate $\rho\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.40,1.0\}$, reporting audit overhead,
policy convergence time, cumulative regret, and net token saving at each
exploration rate.
The curve of net saving versus $\rho$ directly answers the reviewer question
of how much full-trajectory compute must be consumed to make the adaptive
policy worthwhile.

\emph{(A8) Forced-termination vs.\ independent generation.}
Forced checkpoint termination (one long trajectory probed at intermediate
budgets) and independently generated fixed-budget trajectories may produce
different answer distributions; this is a measurement-validity concern
rather than a method comparison.
We evaluate Brier agreement, flip rates, and budget-curve shape under both
protocols on a held-out subset.
This ablation is placed in the appendix unless systematic divergence is
found.

\subsection{Preliminary Budget Audit}
\label{sec:pilot}

To assess whether a budget-selection opportunity is present at all before
committing to the full evaluation, we ran a balanced pilot on 80~items drawn
equally from all four evaluation datasets (20~items per dataset) using
Qwen3-8B on the RTX~4090 under the same BF16 / forced-checkpoint protocol
as the main corpus.  The purpose is to validate the research premise---that
budget effects on predictive quality are non-trivial and heterogeneous---not
to report final results.

Table~\ref{tab:pilot-budget} shows the summed multiclass Brier score at the
lowest budget ($b=256$) and at the per-dataset best-fixed budget for each
dataset.  Brier values are not directly comparable across rows because the
number of answer options differs ($n_\text{opt}=9$--$10$ for MMLU-Pro
vs.\ $n_\text{opt}=4$ for the remaining three datasets).
Budget-response behaviour differed across datasets.
On MMLU-Pro, Brier decreased monotonically from $b=256$ to $b=4096$ before
plateauing, with accuracy rising from $0.45$ to $0.80$.
On ARC-Challenge, Brier improved sharply between $b=256$ and $b=512$ then
plateaued, with the best-fixed budget at the maximum grid point.
On MedMCQA, aggregate Brier worsened with increasing budget---the
best-fixed budget was $b=256$---while accuracy remained largely flat.
On MedQA-USMLE, Brier showed a non-monotone pattern, dropping sharply only
at $b=4096$.
These patterns are preliminary and based on 20~items per dataset; they
illustrate that budget effects are not uniformly positive and motivate
measuring them empirically rather than assuming a direction.

The late-budget increase in NLL on several datasets---despite stable or
improving accuracy---suggests that extreme probability errors may not move
in the same direction as aggregate accuracy; a full analysis is deferred to
the complete corpus.  Answer-transition counts from the pilot also showed
both correct-to-wrong and wrong-to-correct flips at intermediate budget
edges, indicating that aggregate accuracy gains can mask opposing item-level
dynamics of continuation.

An item-adaptive oracle that selects the budget minimising per-item Brier was
computed for each dataset.  The best-fixed budget varied across datasets,
ranging from $b=256$ (MedMCQA) to $b=8192$ (ARC-Challenge, MedQA-USMLE).
Within each dataset, oracle-optimal budgets were distributed across the full
grid rather than concentrated at a single point, suggesting preliminary
item-level heterogeneity.  Oracle Brier reductions relative to the best-fixed
budget ranged from approximately $0.5\%$ (ARC-Challenge, where the best-fixed
budget is near a Brier floor) to approximately $47\%$ (MedQA-USMLE), with
MMLU-Pro at $18\%$ and MedMCQA at $30\%$.
These oracle values are an upper bound on the opportunity available to a
deployable adaptive policy and are not a performance claim for VISTA.

\paragraph{A1 --- Audit weighting.}
At audit rate $\rho=0.20$ the IPW policy risk estimate at $b=256$
is $+1.61$ relative to reference $b=4096$, while the naive unweighted
estimate is $+0.32$, a ratio of $4.96 \approx 1/\rho$.
This is consistent with the mechanical effect of omitting the
$1/\rho$ weight: the naive estimator scales down every observation by $\rho$,
attenuating the estimated risk difference in proportion to the audit rate.
IPW recovers the intended full-population estimand.
A stronger test comparing IPW against a hold-out of \emph{naturally stopped}
trajectories (rather than uniformly audited ones) is pending.

\paragraph{A2 and A7 --- Sequential monitoring.}
Across 200~stream-order permutations at $\rho=0.20$, the greedy monitor
selects a policy worse than the reference in 7.0\% of replays, while the
CS-based monitor records zero false-positive selections at every tested
$\rho \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40, 1.00\}$.
Greedy false-positive rate increases monotonically as $\rho$ decreases:
25.0\% at $\rho=0.05$, 18.5\% at $\rho=0.10$, 7.0\% at $\rho=0.20$,
2.0\% at $\rho=0.40$, and 0\% at $\rho=1.00$ (full trajectory observation).
These results are consistent with intended anytime-valid behaviour.
The result covers a single seed (200 permutations); repeated stream-order
simulations across 100--1000 permutations are needed to distinguish the
theorem guarantee from its empirical validation.

\paragraph{A3 --- Prequential update.}
Under stationary DS1~replay, the prequential policy accumulates mean Brier
regret $+0.1524$ per item versus $+0.1577$ for the frozen fixed-calibration
policy ($\Delta=0.0053$ Brier units).
The two methods behave similarly under stationarity.
Controlled distribution-shift experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:shift}) are the decisive test; the fixed-calibration
policy is expected to degrade when its calibration set becomes
unrepresentative.

\paragraph{A4 --- Prediction objective.}
On DS1, accuracy and Brier both select $b=8192$ as the optimal fixed budget
($0.738$ accuracy; Brier $0.4917$), while NLL selects $b=2048$ ($2.458$~nats,
compared to $2.763$ at $b=8192$).
Different predictive objectives can prefer different reasoning budgets on
the same dataset; the practical significance depends on the downstream
decision task.
The confident-wrong rate (items where $\max_k \hat{p}_k \geq 0.90$ and
the prediction is incorrect, $\tau=0.90$) is $19.3\%$ at $b=256$ and
\emph{increases} to $21.6\%$ at $b=8192$.
At higher budgets the model becomes more extreme in its predictions: it is
more confident when correct, but also more confident when incorrect.
This is consistent with the NLL worsening at $b=8192$ despite accuracy
improving.

\paragraph{A5 --- Allocation granularity.}
The best fixed budget on DS1 is $b=8192$ (mean Brier $0.4917$).
The hindsight per-item oracle---which assigns each item the budget minimising
its individual Brier score---achieves mean Brier $0.3437$, a $30.1\%$
relative reduction.
This oracle is not deployable: it requires the correct answer at prediction
time and serves as an upper bound only.

The matched-quality token oracle---which assigns each item the \emph{minimum}
budget $b$ such that its Brier is no worse than the global best-fixed Brier
($0.4917$)---achieves mean budget ${\approx}2552$ tokens, compared to the
best-fixed $b=8192$ ($31\%$ of maximum).
On $76.1\%$ of DS1 items a budget strictly smaller than $b=8192$ already
meets the quality threshold.
This result confirms Gate~0 on DS1: a substantial compute reduction is
available in principle.
Whether a deployable item-adaptive signal can identify which items need fewer
tokens remains to be tested.

\section{Discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{DS1 preliminary interpretation.}
The DS1 component analysis suggests that individual elements of VISTA
address qualitatively different failure modes: audit weighting corrects a
systematic estimator attenuation that scales with $1/\rho$; sequential
monitoring prevents premature policy switching under low audit rates;
prequential updating provides modest gains under stationarity and its value
is expected primarily under distribution shift; different predictive
objectives select different budgets for the same data, making objective
choice non-trivial; and substantial item-level headroom exists relative to
any fixed-budget policy.
These results are individually promising but do not yet support a unified
claim about end-to-end system performance.
DS2--DS4 results and shift experiments are required before drawing
cross-dataset conclusions.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Assumption~\ref{asm:feedback} restricts applicability to tasks with eventually
verifiable outcomes. The policy family is finite and pre-specified, so
guarantees concern only included candidates. Forced checkpoint evaluation is an
explicit intervention, and results are interpreted within that measurement
regime. Experiments are limited to models that fit on single-workstation GPUs;
whether patterns change at larger scales is an open question. Finally,
randomized auditing incurs real compute costs, which may conflict with
per-request latency constraints even when the long-run value is positive.

\section{Conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We study test-time reasoning-budget selection in a deployment regime with no
held-out calibration set but delayed verifiable feedback. Three interlocking
obstacles---correctness non-identifiability, the missing continuation
counterfactual, and the absence of a calibration split---motivate the
\emph{continuation value} as the decision target and the VISTA
prequential framework as the evaluation procedure. VISTA combines randomized
full-trajectory audits, candidate policy reconstruction, delayed labels, and
anytime-valid confidence sequences without reserving a separate split or
updating model parameters.
